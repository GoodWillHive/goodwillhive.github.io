## Abstract

Each year in the United States, ~3500 infants die of sleep-related infant
deaths, including SIDS (ICD-10 R95), ill-defined deaths (R99), and accidental
suffocation and strangulation in bed (W75). After a substantial decline in the
1990s, the overall death rate has remained stagnant since 2000, and disparities
persist.

The triple risk model proposes that SIDS occurs when an infant with intrinsic
vulnerability (often impaired arousal, cardiorespiratory and/or autonomic
responses) undergoes an exogenous trigger event (eg, an unsafe sleeping
environment) during a critical developmental period.

Core of the recommendation: supine positioning; a firm, noninclined sleep
surface; room sharing without bed sharing; and avoidance of soft bedding and
overheating. Additional: human milk feeding; avoidance of nicotine, alcohol,
marijuana, opioids and illicit drugs; routine immunization; and pacifier use.

New in 2022: noninclined sleep surfaces, short-term emergency sleep locations,
cardboard boxes as a sleep location, bed sharing, substance use, home
cardiorespiratory monitors, and tummy time.

## Terms

- **SIDS** — cause assigned to an infant death that cannot be explained after a
  thorough investigation including scene investigation, autopsy and clinical
  history review.
- **Unexplained sudden death in infancy** — the terminology preferred by the
  National Association of Medical Examiners, in place of SIDS.
- **SUID** — any sudden and unexpected death in infancy, explained or not.
- **Bed sharing / surface sharing** — parent and infant sleeping together on any
  surface (bed, couch, chair). Medical examiners prefer "surface sharing".
- **Room sharing** — parent and infant in the same room, on separate surfaces.
- **Cosleeping** — deprecated; it is used variably for both room sharing and
  surface sharing, so it lacks clarity.
- **Wedging / entrapment** — nose and mouth or thorax compressed between
  objects. The common scenario is an infant stuck between a mattress and a wall
  or bedframe in an adult bed.

Note on evidence: there are no randomised controlled trials in this field, so
case-control studies are the best evidence available.

## Table 2 — Recommendations, with strength

### Level A (consistent, good-quality, patient-oriented evidence)

- Back to sleep for every sleep.
- Use a firm, flat, noninclined sleep surface to reduce the risk of suffocation
  or wedging/entrapment.
- Feeding of human milk is recommended because it is associated with a reduced
  risk of SIDS.
- Infants should sleep in the parents' room, close to the parents' bed, but on a
  separate surface designed for infants, ideally for at least the first 6 months.
- Keep soft objects — pillows, pillow-like toys, quilts, comforters, mattress
  toppers, fur-like materials — and loose bedding such as blankets and nonfitted
  sheets away from the sleep area.
- Offering a pacifier at naptime and bedtime is recommended.
- Avoid smoke and nicotine exposure during pregnancy and after birth.
- Avoid alcohol, marijuana, opioids and illicit drug use during pregnancy and
  after birth.
- Avoid overheating and head covering.
- Pregnant people should obtain regular prenatal care.
- Immunise infants per AAP and CDC guidelines.
- **Do not** use home cardiorespiratory monitors as a strategy to reduce SIDS
  risk.
- Supervised, awake tummy time, beginning soon after hospital discharge,
  increasing incrementally to **at least 15–30 min total daily by age 7 weeks**.
- Clinicians, hospital staff and child care providers must endorse and model the
  guidelines from the beginning of pregnancy.
- Media and manufacturers should follow the guidelines in messaging and
  advertising.
- Continue the NICHD "Safe to Sleep" campaign.

### Level B (inconsistent or limited-quality evidence)

- Avoid commercial devices inconsistent with safe sleep recommendations.

### Level C (consensus, usual practice, opinion)

- **There is no evidence to recommend swaddling as a strategy to reduce SIDS
  risk.**
- Continue research and surveillance.

## Table 3 — What changed since the 2016 guidelines

**Sleep surface**
- 2016: "use a firm sleep surface". 2022: "firm, **flat, noninclined**".
- **Sleep surfaces with inclines of more than 10 degrees are unsafe.**
- Any alternative sleep surface must meet the June 2021 CPSC rule requiring
  infant sleep products to meet existing federal standards for cribs, bassinets,
  play yards and bedside sleepers. This covers inclined sleep products, hammocks,
  baby boxes, in-bed sleepers, baby nests and pods, compact bassinets without a
  stand, travel bassinets and baby tents.
- In an emergency, an alternative with a firm, flat, noninclined surface (box,
  basket, dresser drawer) with thin firm padding may be used temporarily.
- Cradleboards: no safety data; NICHD suggests them as a culturally appropriate
  surface for some American Indian/Alaska Native communities, with care against
  overheating from overbundling.

**Feeding**
- 2016 said "breastfeeding". 2022 says **"feeding of human milk"** —
  exclusively for ~6 months, continuing for 1 year or longer as mutually desired.
- Preterm and low birth weight infants are at higher risk of SIDS, so human milk
  matters more for them.

**Sleep location**
- 2016: room share ideally for the first year, at least 6 months.
  2022: **ideally for at least the first 6 months.**
- 2016 listed bed-sharing circumstances "to be avoided at all times".
  **2022 is unable to recommend bed sharing under any circumstances**, and
  instead publishes the magnitude of added risk (see below).
- Falling asleep with the infant in an adult bed is **relatively less hazardous
  (but still not recommended)** than on a sofa or armchair.
- Cobedding twins and higher-order multiples: any potential benefit is
  outweighed by the risk.

**Soft bedding**
- New: **weighted blankets, weighted sleepers, weighted swaddles or other
  weighted objects must not be placed on or near a sleeping infant.**
- 2016 preferred a wearable blanket. 2022 prefers **layers of clothing**;
  wearable blankets can also be used.

**Pacifier**
- For breastfed infants, delay introduction until breastfeeding is firmly
  established — now defined as **sufficient milk supply; consistent, comfortable
  and effective latch for milk transfer; and appropriate weight gain against
  normative growth curves.** The time required is variable.

**Substances**
- 2016: avoid smoke. 2022: avoid **smoke and nicotine**.
- 2016: avoid alcohol and illicit drugs. 2022 adds **marijuana and opioids**.

**Overheating and head covering**
- New: given the questionable benefit of hats for preventing hypothermia and the
  risk of overheating, **do not place hats on infants indoors**, except in the
  first hours of life or in the NICU.

**Home monitors**
- Direct-to-consumer heart rate and pulse oximetry devices, including wearables,
  are sold as consumer wellness devices. By their FDA designation they are **not
  to be used to prevent sleep-related deaths**. There is concern that they lead
  to parental complacency and reduced adherence to the guidelines.

**Tummy time**
- 2016 had no numbers. 2022: short periods beginning soon after hospital
  discharge, **increasing incrementally to at least 15–30 min total daily by age
  7 weeks**.

**Swaddling**
- New: weighted swaddle clothing or weighted objects within swaddles are unsafe.
- **When an infant shows signs of attempting to roll — usually at 3–4 months but
  possibly earlier — swaddling is no longer appropriate**, because it increases
  the suffocation risk if the swaddled infant rolls prone.

## The recommendations in full

### 1. Back to sleep for every sleep

Supine for every sleep by every caregiver until 1 year of age. **Side sleeping
is not safe and is not advised.**

**Supine does not increase the risk of choking or aspiration**, and is
recommended even for infants with gastro-oesophageal reflux. The infant airway
anatomy and protective mechanisms (eg, gag reflex) protect against aspiration.

> When an infant is on their back, the trachea lies **on top of** the
> oesophagus. Anything regurgitated or refluxed must work against gravity to be
> aspirated. When the infant is on their stomach, anything refluxed pools at the
> opening of the trachea, making it easier to aspirate or choke.
> — figure caption, NICHD Safe to Sleep

AAP concurs with NASPGHAN: no position other than supine is recommended, and
**positional therapy — head elevation, lateral or prone positioning — should not
be used to treat reflux symptoms in sleeping infants.** Elevating the head of
the crib is ineffective and is not recommended.

Infants cannot be placed at a 30-degree incline without sliding down. At lesser
inclines they can more easily flex the trunk and lift the head, which
facilitates rolling to the side or prone — at which point they are at higher
risk of muscle fatigue and suffocation.

Preterm infants: place supine as soon as clinically stable, usually by 32 weeks
postmenstrual age, so that they acclimate to supine sleep before discharge.

Skin-to-skin care is recommended for all mothers and newborns immediately after
birth, for at least an hour. When the parent needs to sleep, the infant goes
supine into a noninclined bassinet.

Infants who can roll both ways may be left in the position they assume — but
because rolling into soft bedding is an important risk factor, **the sleep
environment should be clear of everything but a fitted sheet.**

### 2. Firm, flat, noninclined surface

- Inclines of more than 10 degrees are unsafe.
- A firm surface does not indent or conform to the shape of the head.
- Only mattresses designed for the specific product. No pillows or cushions as
  substitutes. **No mattress toppers under 1 year.** A waterproof cover must be
  thin and tightly fitting. Nothing under or over the mattress to create an
  angle.
- Soft, adjustable-firmness or memory-foam mattresses are dangerous: they create
  a pocket and increase rebreathing or suffocation risk if the infant rolls prone.
- Use a crib, bassinet, portable crib or play yard meeting CPSC standards; check
  cpsc.gov for recalls, especially for used cribs. Do not use a crib with missing
  hardware or missing instructions, and do not attempt repairs.
- **Do not place infants for sleep on adult beds or mattresses** — entrapment and
  suffocation risk. No portable bed rails.
- Keep dangling cords, electric wires and window covering cords out of the sleep
  area.
- **Sitting devices — car seats, strollers, swings, infant carriers, slings — are
  not recommended for routine sleep**, particularly under 4 months. Move a
  sleeping infant to a firm flat surface as soon as safe and practical. Car seats
  are not stable on a crib mattress or other elevated surface. Never leave an
  infant unattended, or with straps unbuckled or partly buckled.
- In slings and cloth carriers: head up and above the fabric, face visible, nose
  and mouth clear. Reposition after nursing.

### 3. Human milk

Breastfeeding is associated with reduced SIDS risk. The protective effect
**increases with exclusivity and with duration**. Any human milk is more
protective than none.

### 4. Room sharing, not bed sharing

**Sleeping in the parents' room on a separate surface decreases SIDS risk by as
much as 50%.** Infants sleeping in a separate room are **2.75 to 11.5 times**
more likely to die suddenly and unexpectedly than infants room sharing without
bed sharing. Across all bed-sharing circumstances in meta-analysis, the risk is
almost **3 times** that of room sharing without bed sharing.

Return an infant brought into the adult bed for feeding or comforting to their
own crib when the parent is ready to sleep.

> **Couches and armchairs are extremely dangerous places for infants and should
> never be used for infant sleep.** Sleeping there places infants at
> extraordinarily high risk — **22- to 67-fold increased risk** — of death,
> through entrapment or wedging between seat cushions, or overlay.

AAP understands and respects that many parents choose to bed share, for
breastfeeding, cultural preference, or the belief it is safer. Based on the
evidence it is nonetheless unable to recommend bed sharing under any
circumstances. It publishes the magnitudes so that families and clinicians can
weigh specific situations:

**More than 10× the baseline risk of parent–infant bed sharing**
- Bed sharing with someone impaired in alertness or ability to arouse because of
  fatigue, sedating medication (certain antidepressants, pain medication) or
  substances (alcohol, illicit drugs).
- Bed sharing with a current smoker **even if that person does not smoke in
  bed**, or if the pregnant parent smoked during pregnancy.
- Bed sharing on a soft surface: waterbed, old mattress, sofa, couch, armchair.

**5–10×**
- A term, normal weight infant **under 4 months**, even if neither parent smokes
  and even if the infant is breastfed. This is a particularly vulnerable time.
- Bed sharing with anyone who is not the infant's parent, including nonparental
  caregivers and other children.

**2–5×**
- Preterm or low birth weight infant, even if neither parent smokes.
- Soft bedding accessories such as pillows or blankets.

Unintentional bed sharing happens when parents fall asleep feeding, or when they
are particularly tired or the infant is fussy. A large percentage of infants who
die while bed sharing are found with the face or head covered by bedding — so no
pillows, sheets, blankets, pets or other soft or loose items in the bed. **The
risk rises with the duration of bed sharing**, so return the infant to a separate
surface as soon as the parent wakes.

### 5. Soft objects and loose bedding

Airway obstruction from soft objects or loose bedding is **the most common
mechanism of accidental infant suffocation**. The other two mechanisms are
wedging/entrapment between two objects, and overlay.

- No weighted blankets, weighted sleepers, weighted swaddles or weighted objects
  on or near the sleeping infant.
- Dress in layers rather than using blankets; wearable blankets are acceptable.
- **No bumper pads** or similar products attaching to crib slats: implicated in
  deaths from suffocation, entrapment and strangulation, and unnecessary under
  current slat standards.

### 6. Pacifier

Offer at naptime and bedtime. **For breastfed infants, delay until breastfeeding
is firmly established** — sufficient milk supply; consistent, comfortable and
effective latch for milk transfer; appropriate weight gain. Infants not directly
breastfed may begin as soon as desired.

The protective effect is observed **even if the pacifier falls out** of the mouth.
It does not need to be reinserted once asleep. Do not force it on an infant who
refuses; try again when older.

Never hang a pacifier around the neck or attach it to clothing during sleep —
strangulation risk. Never attach blankets or plush toys to pacifiers.

There is insufficient evidence that finger sucking is protective.

### 7–8. Smoke, nicotine, alcohol and drugs

Both smoking in pregnancy and smoke in the infant's environment after birth are
major risk factors. No evidence yet on vaping and SUID, but electronic
cigarettes contain nicotine, which has been implicated. Risk is particularly
high when the infant bed shares with an adult smoker **even when that adult does
not smoke in bed**.

Alcohol, marijuana, opioid and illicit drug use periconceptionally and during
pregnancy is strongly advised against. Risk is significantly higher with
concomitant smoking and alcohol use. Parental substance use **combined with bed
sharing** puts the infant at particularly high risk.

### 9. Overheating and head covering

The definition of overheating varies between studies, so no specific room
temperature can be given.

- Dress the infant for the environment, **no more than one layer more than an
  adult** would wear to be comfortable there.
- Signs of overheating: sweating, flushed skin, chest hot to the touch.
- Avoid overbundling and covering of the face and head. **Do not place hats on
  infants indoors**, except in the first hours of life or in the NICU.
- Insufficient evidence to recommend a fan as a risk-reduction strategy.

### 11. Immunisation

There is no evidence of a causal relationship between immunisation and SIDS.
**Vaccination may have a protective effect.**

### 12–13. Commercial devices and home monitors

Be wary of devices claiming to reduce SIDS risk; none is proven, and such claims
create a false sense of security. Home cardiorespiratory monitoring has not been
documented to decrease SIDS incidence, and in-hospital monitoring before
discharge does not detect infants at risk. A family's decision to use a monitor
is not a substitute for following the guidelines.

### 14. Tummy time

Supervised, awake tummy time to facilitate development and minimise positional
plagiocephaly: short periods beginning soon after hospital discharge, increasing
incrementally to **at least 15–30 minutes total daily by 7 weeks of age**.

### 15. Swaddling

**There is no evidence to recommend swaddling as a strategy to reduce SIDS
risk.** It is used to calm the infant and to encourage supine positioning.

- **High risk of death if a swaddled infant is placed prone or rolls prone.**
- If swaddled, always place on the back.
- **Snug around the chest but with ample room at the hips and knees**, to avoid
  exacerbating hip dysplasia.
- Weighted swaddle clothing and weighted objects within swaddles are not safe.
- Stop when the infant shows signs of attempting to roll — usually 3–4 months,
  but possibly earlier.
- No evidence either way on arms in versus arms out; parents may decide.

## Reader correspondence

Four comments were published. Two are substantive and are the live dispute in
this field.

### Spanish Breastfeeding Association (Miguel Menéndez), 25 July 2022

Argues the **strength-of-recommendation grades are wrong**. Under SORT, grade A
requires consistent, good-quality evidence from well-designed prospective
studies; the bed-sharing recommendation rests on case-control studies and a
meta-analysis of case-control studies. That meta-analysis found **no significant
increase in risk among non-smoking parents who bed shared (OR 1.66, 95% CI
0.91–3.01)**. They disagree that bed sharing should be avoided in all cases:
bed sharing is associated with longer breastfeeding, and without other risk
factors current evidence does not support asserting increased risk.

### AAP Task Force reply, 4 August 2022

SORT explicitly allows "all-or-none studies" into grade A — cases where the
treatment causes a dramatic change in outcome and a controlled trial is
precluded. Sleep-related infant death meets that, since randomising prone versus
supine or bedsharing versus not is impossible.

On the evidence: the vast majority of bed-sharing studies show statistically
significant increased risk even after adjustment, including for breastfeeding.
Before the 2016 guidelines, an independent biostatistician (Robert Platt)
reviewed the two studies that reached opposing conclusions — Blair 2014 (2 UK
case-control studies) and Carpenter 2013 (19 studies, UK/Europe/Australasia) —
and concluded:

> "There is some evidence of an increased risk in the no-other-risk-factor
> setting, in particular in the youngest age groups. However, based on concerns
> about sample size limitations, we are not able to say how large that increased
> risk is. Clearly, these data do not support a definitive conclusion that
> bed-sharing in the youngest age group is safe, even under less hazardous
> circumstances."

### Bartick, McKenna, Young, 1 July 2022 — "breastsleeping"

Objects to the switch from "breastfeeding" to "human milk feeding". Only ~5.6%
of US mothers exclusively pump, so studies could not have had the power to show
that human milk *without* direct breastfeeding is protective.

Their mechanism claim: breastfeeding dyads behave differently. Bedsharing
breastfeeding mothers and infants are more arousable; the mother forms a
protective circle with arms and legs shielding the infant from her pillow, a
behaviour seen less in mothers who never breastfed; the infant rolls onto their
back after feeding rather than staying prone. Videographic data show formula
feeding mothers placing infants on pillows and in other unsafe positions.

They add that populations with high bedsharing rates often have low
sleep-related death rates, correlating with low population rates of hazardous
factors — suggesting the hazards, not bedsharing itself, drive the deaths.

### Halbower and Stenmark, 14 July 2022

The SUID incidence curve has flattened despite decades of guidelines and
education. Parental attitudes and beliefs determine which recommendations get
followed. Culturally sensitive, novel approaches may do more than more
publication.

Corrected two errors: the pacifier recommendation omitted the delay for
breastfed infants, and the swaddling stop age was given as 2–3 months instead of
3–4 months or earlier.

Added: the bed-sharing risk tiers with magnitudes, the 22–67× figure for sofas,
the ~50% reduction from room sharing and 2.75–11.5× for a separate room,
weighted-object ban, no hats indoors, layers rather than blankets, and the
